I Gave Myself Six Weeks to Build an App. But It's Already Playable.

What roughly two dozen hours of AI-assisted development actually looks like—and why I'm not calling it finished.

By Jenna Clark · Draft for November 2026

Shortly after I announced my plan to spend six weeks building a digital version of our family's Christmas game, my friend Emily sent me a message.

“You don't need six weeks to build that!”

Which was funny, because I'd just sent her a link to play it.

“Emily, you've already played the game.”

“Oh, yeah.”

And that got me thinking.

If the game is already playable, what exactly am I spending six weeks doing?

How long did it actually take?

When I announced the six-week experiment, I left out one fairly important detail: I'd already been building the game, on and off, since September.

And by the end of October, it was a functioning multiplayer app.

People could join from their phones, guess product prices, reveal the answers, and compete for points. We had a hosted game, a growing library of real Argos products, and even an optional TV scoreboard.

I'm not a traditional software developer, so seeing something I'd imagined become playable that quickly was genuinely exciting.

But I also wanted to understand how quickly it had actually happened. Not how long AI took to generate code. How much time had I spent thinking, making decisions, working with AI, gathering content, designing, testing, troubleshooting, and getting everything online?

Fortunately, I'd been keeping a build log. Here's how it unfolded.

From idea to playable game

  1. SEP 21–22

    The idea

    The phone-first guessing game, scoring direction, and first product links. Nothing built yet.

  2. OCT 21

    Eight items, simulated players, and a design inspired by the original kraft envelope and handwritten scoring sheets.

  3. OCT 28

  4. OCT 29

    Real multiplayer: four-digit room codes, live guesses, price reveals, scoring, three rounds, a hosted game, and an optional TV scoreboard.

  5. NOV 2

    A phone reload wiped guesses. We fixed rejoining, simplified the flow, made the guessing screen clearer, and added scheduling across time zones.

  6. NOV 4

    Practice and polish

    Computer opponents, clearer lobby feedback, a host notification when someone joins—and the launch of this public build journal.

The numbers surprised me

I went back through the build log and estimated how much hands-on time I'd spent on the different parts of the project.

What I was doingEstimated time
Conceiving2h
Building (product development)8.5h
Content & product data5h
Design & branding3h
Testing & QA4h
Shipping & hosting1.5h
Documentation2h
Total estimated~26h

These aren't stopwatch measurements. They're estimates from a working log, with some overlapping activities. The dated sessions add up to about 22 hours, while the category estimates total roughly 26. I haven't reconciled the difference to the half-hour.

But the broader picture is interesting. Only about a third of the estimated time was spent directly building the app and its features. The rest went into decisions, content, design, testing, and operational work surrounding the code.

AI dramatically accelerated the implementation. It didn't make the rest of the work disappear.

The prototype is not the product

This is where I think the conversation about building with AI gets especially interesting.

For someone like me, who has spent a career turning ideas into systems and products but isn't a traditional developer, being able to work directly on a functioning application is extraordinary.

I can describe the experience I want, make decisions about how it should work, test what comes back, and iterate.

Claude has done the technical heavy lifting: building the multiplayer functionality, integrating the real-time database, implementing the game mechanics, and helping fix what breaks.

My role has been deciding what we're building, how the experience should work, what needs changing, and whether the result is actually good enough.

And the speed is remarkable. But when I sent the game to my family, I was reminded of something that hasn't changed.

Getting software to work is one milestone. Getting it to work reliably for other people is another.

A game that loses someone's guesses when they refresh technically works—until someone refreshes.

A guessing screen technically works—even if someone can't find the submit button.

A multiplayer room technically works—but if a player doesn't know whether they're waiting for the host or experiencing a technical problem, the experience still needs work.

Those details aren't exciting AI demos. They're what make something usable.

So what are the six weeks actually for?

They're not six weeks of generating more code simply because I can. They're time to make sure the game is ready for the people it's meant for.

Reliability. What happens when someone's phone disconnects? Can they rejoin without losing their guesses? What happens when the whole family joins at once?

Testing. What happens when people who haven't spent hours thinking about the game try to play it? Which assumptions turn out to be wrong?

Thoughtful design. Can everyone understand what to do? Is it obvious when to guess, submit, wait, and reveal?

Accessibility. Can people with different abilities, devices, and levels of technical confidence participate? What still needs improvement?

Content accuracy. Are the product photos reliable? Are the prices still current? Will they be accurate when Christmas arrives?

And perhaps most importantly: Does it actually feel like our family game?

Does it preserve the laughter, the competitive guessing, the ridiculous answers, and the little traditions that have made it part of Christmas for nearly 30 years?

Because the goal was never simply to build an app. The goal was to keep a family tradition going.

What AI actually changes

One of the reasons I wanted to document this experiment is that I'm interested in how AI changes the work of building things. Not just the speed of development, but where the time, effort, and judgment go.

AI has made technical implementation dramatically more accessible to me. But it hasn't eliminated the need for sound architecture, testing, thoughtful design, or human judgment.

If anything, the faster you can create something, the more important those decisions become.

When you can build another feature in minutes, you have to be deliberate about whether it belongs. When you can produce a working prototype quickly, you have to resist mistaking that speed for a finished product.

And when the technical barriers get lower, the questions shift.

Should we build it this way?
Will it work when real people use it?
Does it actually make their experience better?

Those are questions I want to keep exploring over the next several weeks.

Back to Emily

So yes, Emily. You were right. I didn't need six more weeks to get a working version of the game into your hands. You'd already played it.

But I'm giving myself the time to make sure our family can enjoy it on Christmas Day—across three countries, on different phones, with all the unpredictability that comes with getting everyone together.

AI shortened the time it took to build the game. It hasn't shortened the list of things that make the game worth playing.

And I think that's where the more interesting work begins.
