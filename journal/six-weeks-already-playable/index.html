I Gave Myself Six Weeks to Build an App. The Prototype Took a Day.

Around 30 hours building a Christmas game with AI—and why playable isn't the same as finished.

By Jenna Clark · Draft for November 2026
Uses your device's voice

The day after I announced my plan to build a digital version of our family's Christmas game, I got a text from my friend Emily.

“You don't need six weeks to build that!”

Which was funny, because I'd just sent her a link to play it.

“Emily, you've already played the game.”

“Oh, yeah.”

When I announced the experiment, I left out one fairly important detail: I'd already been working on the game, on and off, since September.

I'd come up with the idea, put it aside for a month, and picked it back up in October. The first clickable prototype came together in a day. Over the next few sessions, it became a multiplayer game with real products, phone-based guessing, scoring, and a shared TV scoreboard.

It wasn't finished. But it worked.

There's something I've noticed about building with AI: it's remarkably easy to lose track of time.

You start with one small change. Then you see something else you could improve. Claude makes the update, you test it, spot another possibility, and off you go again. Suddenly you've spent an entire evening tinkering with something you thought would take twenty minutes.

It's fun. And it's a reminder that fast implementation doesn't necessarily mean less time spent making decisions. In fact, when I added everything up, I'd spent around 30 hours working on the game—and only about a third of that time was directly building it. The rest went into product decisions, design, finding and organizing content, testing, troubleshooting, and getting it into people's hands.

AI makes it possible to build a convincing prototype incredibly quickly. But that speed can create the illusion that the product is finished.

The mother-in-law test

Once the game was working, I had a decision to make.

I'd originally planned to surprise my mother-in-law with it at Christmas. But she's been the mastermind behind this tradition for 30 years, and she'd wanted an app version for ages.

It seemed a little ridiculous to build the whole thing without her. Besides, you have to know your audience.

So we brought her into the process and played together as a family.

We found bugs almost immediately: refreshing a phone could wipe out someone's guesses, joining a game wasn't always intuitive, and we needed a better way to organize games across time zones.

The test gave us something more useful than a successful demo: a list of things to fix from the people who would actually be playing.

Along the way, the game acquired a new name: Clarkos. Clark family meets Argos. A terrible pun, which naturally made it irresistible.

Let's take a look inside Clarkos

Here's the funny thing about having a working app: people can see it, press the buttons, play the game, and reasonably wonder what else there is to do.

But the family test had already shown how much was left to get right.

I could ask AI to build a Christmas price-guessing game, and it could. But it wouldn't automatically know the little things that make it our game: the history, the personalities, the way we've played for years, or why my mother-in-law has kept the tradition going for three decades.

That context matters. So does bringing the people you're building for into the process.

After years of building teams, systems, and better ways of working inside large organizations, I'm seeing how much of that same thinking applies here.

AI can help write code, develop requirements, design interfaces, and test solutions. But the ability to build something isn't the same as knowing what to build—or why.

A tool can make a process faster without making the process better. You still need to understand the problem, the people involved, and what you're trying to achieve.

The next few weeks are about listening to the people playing, fixing what doesn't work, and making Clarkos something my family genuinely enjoys.

Emily was right. I didn't need six more weeks to get a working game.

But I wasn't trying to build just any game. I wanted to help keep a 30-year-old family tradition going.

AI shortened the time it took to build the game. It hasn't shortened the list of things that make the game worth playing.
