I Gave Myself Six Weeks to Build an App. But It's Already Playable.

About 26 hours of building with AI—and why playable isn't the same as finished.

By Jenna Clark · Draft for November 2026

Shortly after I announced my plan to spend six weeks building a digital version of our family's Christmas game, my friend Emily sent me a message.

“You don't need six weeks to build that!”

Which was funny, because I'd just sent her a link to play it.

“Emily, you've already played the game.”

“Oh, yeah.”

And that got me thinking.

If the game is already playable, what exactly am I spending six weeks doing?

How long did it actually take?

When I announced the six-week experiment, I left out one fairly important detail: I'd already been working on the game, on and off, since September.

By the end of October, it was a functioning multiplayer game. People could join from their phones, guess product prices, reveal answers, and compete for points. We had a hosted game, a growing library of real Argos products, and an optional TV scoreboard.

One thing I've noticed about building with AI is how easily you can lose track of time.

What starts as a quick experiment turns into hours of tweaking, testing, changing your mind, and trying again. And when someone sees the finished result, it's easy to assume AI did everything.

So I wanted to find out how much time I'd actually spent—and where it went.

Fortunately, I'd been keeping a build log.

From idea to playable game

  1. SEP 21

    The idea

    Worked out the basic play model and scoring, and started collecting products. Nothing built yet. Then I put the idea aside for a month.

  2. OCT 21

    First prototype

    Picked the project back up and built a clickable prototype, with an interface inspired by the original handwritten family game.

  3. OCT 28

    Real products, real personality

    Added Argos-inspired visual styling, built out the product library, and introduced Trevor the dinosaur in a Santa hat. Obviously essential.

  4. OCT 29

    Multiplayer!

    The big leap. Live rooms, phone-based guessing, scoring, three rounds of play, an optional TV scoreboard, and approximately 500 products. The game was online and playable.

  5. NOV 2

    My family broke it

    A phone refresh wiped someone's guesses. Joining a game wasn't always intuitive, and we needed a way to schedule games across time zones. We fixed rejoining, simplified the experience, and added scheduling.

  6. NOV 4

    Practice and polish

    Added computer opponents, improved the lobby, and launched this public build journal.

Where did the time go?

I went back through the build log and estimated how much time I'd spent on each part of the project.

These are approximate estimates based on my build log, not a stopwatch.

Only about a third of that time was spent directly building the app.

The rest went into decisions, design, content, testing, and getting it into people's hands.

And that brings me back to the six weeks.

A working prototype isn't the same thing as a finished product.

My first family playtest made that abundantly clear.

If someone's guesses disappear when they refresh their phone, it doesn't matter how quickly the game was built.

If the instructions aren't clear or the buttons are hard to find, it doesn't matter how clever the technology is.

And if people can't reliably play together from across the world, we've missed the point entirely.

There's still testing to do, accessibility to consider, product prices to check, and an experience to refine.

The next few weeks are about seeing what breaks, listening to the people playing, and making the experience simpler, more reliable, and more fun.

AI has made the technical side of building something dramatically more accessible. Claude has been my primary development partner, handling much of the coding and technical integration, while I've been defining requirements, making product decisions, testing, troubleshooting, and deciding what needs to change.

After years of building systems and processes inside large organizations, it's been fascinating to see how much of that same thinking applies to something as small as a family game.

So Emily was right. I didn't need six more weeks to make the game playable.

But playable was never the goal.

The goal is to keep a 30-year-old family tradition going.

AI shortened the time it took to build the game. It hasn't shortened the list of things that make the game worth playing.
