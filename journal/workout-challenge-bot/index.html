Building for My Toughest Users: My Mom Discord.

A monthly workout and accountability challenge for my mom Discord, with goals, check-ins, mini missions, and plenty of friendly competition.

The problem

Every month, my mom Discord runs a workout and accountability challenge. We set goals, cheer each other on, complete mini missions, and compete for bragging rights. But keeping track of everything was becoming a workout of its own.

Over the past year, we'd experimented with different rules and tried Google Sheets, Forms, and a dedicated fitness app. Every solution meant another place to go and another thing to remember.

Eventually, I realized we didn't need another tracking app. We needed to run the challenge right in Discord, where everyone already was.

So I built a bot.

With Claude helping on the technical side, I worked through our latest rules and built a Python bot for our Discord server. It handles sign-ups, goals, photo check-ins, reminders, daily mini missions, and the leaderboard.

In about two days, I had a working bot. Then my friends started using it, and the real testing began.

One friend struggled to enter her goals on her phone. It had seemed simple enough when I built it, but actually using it was another story. I reworked the flow four times before landing on one form for all three goals. It was a good reminder that just because something works doesn't mean it's easy to use.

The hardest part wasn't the code.

It was figuring out the rules we'd been debating for months.

Some scoring systems rewarded people who chose easier goals. Others made it tempting to give up after missing a day. Claude could update the calculations quickly, but deciding what was fair, and what would actually motivate everyone, took a lot more thought.

We landed on partial credit for progress, rewards for meeting goals, and fun daily mini missions that don't overshadow the challenge. That took more discussion than writing the code, and it's probably the part I'm happiest with.

Testing before launch

One of my favorite parts was simulating a full month before launch. I created fake players, check-ins, missed goals, ties, and other scenarios to see what might break.

We caught a few funny problems: a leaderboard that crowned only one person when there was a tie, and a progress grid that made almost everyone look like they were doing great. Neither would have been obvious from checking whether the code ran.

What I'm taking away

Build where people already are. Moving the challenge into Discord was easier than getting everyone to use another app.

Working isn't the same as usable. I thought goal entry was straightforward until someone actually tried it on a phone.

Keep humans in the loop. AI helped me build and test the bot, but real feedback showed me what needed to change. The people using it understood problems I hadn't anticipated.

Design for behavior, not just functionality. The hardest decisions weren't technical. They were about fairness, motivation, and making sure the challenge encouraged people to keep going.

AI makes experimentation faster. Claude helped me try ideas, test different approaches, and make changes quickly. But faster development doesn't replace judgment. It gives me more time to focus on the people, the experience, and what actually makes something better.

What comes next

We're using the bot for our monthly challenges, and I'm still making improvements as we go. I'm curious whether having everything in Discord helps people participate more consistently, but I don't have enough data to say yet.

For now, we've replaced the spreadsheets, forms, and manual reminders with something we can use right in the conversation. And I'm having a lot of fun building it.
